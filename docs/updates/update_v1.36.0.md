# Mirage v1.36.0 Release Notes

### A way to spend other people's money

The most important thing in this release is a flaw that let one ordinary transaction move the entire balance out of any account that had a username, and post, vote, follow and delete as that person. Mirage lets you act without holding network tokens: your action is wrapped in an envelope you sign with your own key, and a node forwards it and pays the fee. Every check that makes that safe — that the envelope really is yours, that it has not been sent before, that a free account did the small piece of work we ask for — was applied to the actions at the top level of a transaction. It was possible to put those same actions one layer down, inside a standard wrapper the chain already understood, and the checks looked straight past them. Nothing further was needed: no special permission, no cooperation from a node operator, no timing.

It is fixed, and fixed at the level of the flaw rather than the one wrapper that exposed it. The chain now looks through every layer of nesting before deciding what a transaction is, refuses a wrapped envelope action outright, and the wrapper module itself has been removed from Mirage entirely — it was never used for anything. The test that guards this covers every one of the twenty-five action types rather than a sample, and we verified it by reintroducing the flaw and confirming it was caught. This was found by reviewing our own code, not by an incident, but it is the reason this release is mandatory and the reason it goes out as a coordinated upgrade of every node at once.

### A node that would rather stop than be wrong

Two more problems in the same area: a disk fault while reading a list of stored items, or while writing a single value, could be silently discarded. Nothing crashed and nothing was logged. The node simply carried on with an answer that was quietly incomplete, committing a version of history subtly different from every other node's — the hardest kind of fault to diagnose, because by the time anyone notices, the evidence is buried thousands of blocks back. Those faults are now visible at every layer they pass through.

That raised a fair question about what a node should do when it does notice. We settled on a rule and applied it everywhere: while a node is finalising a block, it stops — cleanly, by name, preserving a copy of its state for diagnosis — because continuing would mean writing down something different from everyone else. At every other moment, including while answering ordinary requests, it now returns an error for that one request and stays up. That distinction was previously missing: a momentary disk hiccup while answering somebody's page load could take a validator offline, on a path where nothing was being written down and nothing could have gone wrong. Roughly thirty places behaved that way and none of them do now.

### Settings that cannot break the network

The chain has settings that governance can vote to change, and we found several that could be voted to values which pass every validity check and then break the network. One would have made the small proof-of-work we ask of free accounts mathematically impossible, silently locking out every free user while paying subscribers noticed nothing at all. Two others would have removed the only per-message cost paying accounts bear, and a fourth would have made a subscription charge in full while funding nothing, demoting the subscriber on their very first action. These now have real limits, applied when governance proposes a change rather than when the chain reads a value, so that a node replaying the network's entire history from the beginning still works. We checked the live network against the new limits before shipping: everything is comfortably inside them.

### Two timers that were already counting down

Mirage keeps the chain, which is the record of what happened, and an index built from it, which is what actually answers your feed, your profile and your balances. This release defuses two ways that index could have stopped permanently — not through an attack, but through the platform simply being used. The first was hiding in how the indexer read governance activity: it guessed that the labels attached to each event were encoded, and tried to decode them. Most of the time the guess was harmless. But roughly one proposal number in nine, once numbers reach four digits, happens to look exactly like encoded data and quietly turns into nonsense. The moment that happened, the block would fail, roll back, and be retried forever, on every node at once, with rebuilding from scratch stopping at the very same block. We measured how much time was left: the network is at proposal 108, and the first number that breaks is 1400.

### The second one needed a single ordinary decision

The other timer was simpler and closer. The chain treats anyone at level 100 or above as an administrator, an open-ended range by design. The indexer had been written against a fixed list of levels and knew about exactly 100. Any other administrator level — 101, 150, anything — was a value it had no answer for. It would not have failed at the moment the level was granted; it would have failed later, on the first ordinary post or vote by that account, and then kept failing on that block forever. Nothing about this required bad intent. A routine governance decision to give someone a slightly different administrator level would have been enough, and the damage would have looked like an unexplained outage rather than a consequence of the vote. The indexer now derives administrator status the same way the chain does, from the range rather than a list, so the two can no longer disagree.

### Deleting a post no longer leaves the credit behind

Influence on Mirage is earned per topic: participate in a topic and your votes there carry more weight. That credit was being granted when a post was made and never taken back when the post was deleted, which meant the same effort could be banked over and over — post, self-vote, delete, repeat — until the account had accumulated enough standing in a topic to downvote with full force without ever leaving anything visible behind. It was cheap, it left no trace on the site, and it worked. Deleting a post now withdraws the standing it granted its author, and a repair pass rebuilds the affected histories so that credit banked before this release does not survive it. We want to be straightforward about one deliberate limit: other people who voted on a post keep what they earned when it is deleted. Taking theirs away too would have closed the same hole, but it would have handed authors a new lever — delete your own popular post to strip standing from someone you dislike. We would rather leave a smaller gap than open a way to harm other users.

### An index that refuses to erase itself

While reviewing how the indexer synchronises with the chain at startup, we found that it would faithfully carry out an instruction it should never accept. If the chain reported that it held no user profiles at all — the kind of answer a node gives while it is starting up, misconfigured, or pointed somewhere unexpected — the indexer concluded that every user on the platform had been deleted and marked them accordingly. A momentary bad answer could therefore erase everyone. The synchronisation now refuses an empty answer outright when it already knows about users, and stops if a single pass would remove more than a small fraction of them. A genuinely new network with no users still starts normally, and the ordinary trickle of departing accounts is untouched, so the guard only fires on the shape of an answer that should never be believed.

### Governance actions that cannot go missing quietly

We also closed a gap where the indexer could accept a governance proposal and silently drop part of it. It only recorded the kinds of decisions it already knew how to represent and ignored the rest, so a newly added kind of action would have passed through governance, taken effect on the chain, and left no trace in the record everyone reads. That silence is now an error: an unrecognised Mirage action stops the block loudly instead of disappearing. Alongside it, a handful of smaller robustness fixes went in — the network calls the indexer makes while collecting balances now have an overall time limit rather than only a per-call one, a very deep comment thread can no longer make one block's work grow without bound, an interrupted indexer reports that it was interrupted instead of appearing to have shut down cleanly, and turning the indexer off in configuration now simply turns it off rather than producing a crash loop that looks like a fault.

### One blocked topic could have taken the whole site down

Mirage lets you block a topic using a pattern, so blocking `crypto*` hides everything that starts with it. The site was working out whether a topic matched by a different method than the chain uses, and that method had a nasty property: certain perfectly legal patterns made it take not milliseconds but tens of seconds to decide a single answer — for every post it checked. One block, set once by any ordinary account, was enough to exhaust the server on demand. Worse, the same slowdown sat inside the machinery that delivers notifications, where it needed no further help from anyone: it would jam quietly and then start discarding everybody's queued notifications, for every user on the node, while looking like nothing was wrong. The site now matches topics exactly the way the chain does, which is fast by construction, and we cap how elaborate a new pattern is allowed to be.

### Notifications now respect the blocks you already set

If you blocked someone, you stopped seeing them in the app — but not on your phone. Notifications were sent without ever consulting your block list, so someone you had blocked could still put their words on your lock screen by replying to you or mentioning your name, over and over, while the app itself showed you nothing to report. That is fixed: a notification is now dropped if you, or an assistant you have enabled, has blocked the sender, and if we cannot read your block list we do not send. Notifications also check that the post still exists before going out, so writing something abusive and deleting it seconds later no longer leaves it sitting on someone's phone with no way to trace it. Separately, a single post could previously generate thousands of mention notifications and, in doing so, destroy everyone else's queued ones — those are now capped, and only real accounts count.

### Quest rewards that cannot be claimed twice

Quest completion had a timing flaw: two things happening at nearly the same moment could both conclude a quest was unfinished and both pay out. The same flaw sat in invite rewards, where two people joining with codes from the same person could pay that person twice — or, in an unluckier version, mark the quest complete and then fail before the new user's own reward was written, losing it permanently. Completions are now serialised per account so only one can be in flight at a time. Quest payouts remain switched off, so nothing was lost; this closes the door before they are switched on.

### Claiming rewards no longer looks like it failed

With payouts switched on, claiming told you it had gone wrong. You would tap Claim, the rewards would leave your list, and a red message would tell you the payout was still being confirmed and to check back in a moment. Nothing had actually failed: your rewards were granted the instant you tapped, and the only thing still in progress was the token transfer settling into a block a few seconds later. The app was reporting that short wait as an error. Now a claim is treated as what it is — done — on both web and mobile, and the transfer finishes quietly in the background. The honest caveat is that on the rare occasion a transfer never lands, the rewards return to your list to be claimed again, so a claim you were shown as complete can briefly come back.

### Smaller things that were quietly wrong

A number of limits turned out to be advisory rather than real. Uploads checked their size limit only after the entire file had already been transferred and written to disk, so an image limit of 15 MB actually allowed a gigabyte and a half. Requests for a page of a feed had no upper bound at all, so a single anonymous request could ask for the whole database at once. The "active users" figure on the welcome screen counted anyone who sent a well-formed-looking request, verified or not, meaning it could be inflated freely by someone who never logged in — it now counts only requests that actually succeeded. Searching for a username treated certain punctuation as a wildcard, and one character returned an error page. Database queries had no time limit anywhere, which is what turned each of these from slow into fatal; they now do.

### The recovery phrase actually recovers your account now

If you protected your recovery phrase with a password or a passkey, the unlock screen offered you a way back in — "sign in with recovery phrase instead" — for the case where you had forgotten the password or lost the passkey. It could not work. Ever. The link cleared the key it needed a moment later, and the attempt to store your phrase failed silently: no error, no warning, an app that looked signed in while every single action you took quietly did nothing. For anyone locked out, it was a loop with no way out, and it was the only route back to your own account. It now works. If your vault is locked, your phrase is held in memory for the session so you can use the app immediately, and we tell you plainly that it will not survive a refresh and that you should set your password or passkey up again in Settings. We chose to tell you rather than silently write your phrase to disk in the clear, which would have been the easier fix.

### Seeing your recovery phrase now asks who you are

Your recovery phrase is your account. If you had protected it with a password or a passkey, the Settings page would nonetheless show it to anyone who clicked, with no questions asked — the protection covered storage but not the reveal button. Now it asks. If it has been more than two minutes since you last unlocked, revealing or copying the phrase prompts for your password or re-runs the passkey check first. If you keep the phrase unprotected, which is still the default, nothing changes for you.

### Signing out on a shared computer

Signing out was leaving more behind than it should have. The feed you had been reading, recent responses from the server, and part of the browser's temporary storage all survived, which meant that on a shared computer the next person to sign in could briefly be shown the previous account's personalised feed, complete with which posts that person had voted on, until fresh content replaced it. Everything account-bound is now cleared on sign-out. Signing out in one tab also properly shuts down what the other tabs were doing rather than just locking them, so nothing that was queued before you left can still be running afterwards.

### Invite codes are now signed along with everything else

When you sign up using someone's invite code, that code decides who gets paid a referral reward. The code was being attached to your registration after your signature was calculated, which meant the signature did not actually cover it — anyone able to sit between your browser and Mirage could have swapped in their own code and redirected the reward. The invite code and the referrer's name are now signed with the same key and tied to the same request as everything else, and the server refuses a registration where they do not match. We deliberately limited this to the two fields with money attached rather than signing everything, and we would revisit that immediately if Mirage ever relayed through anyone else's server.

### The browser is now told to enforce, not just observe

Mirage has been shipping a content security policy — the instruction that tells your browser what the page is allowed to load — in observation mode since the day it was added, waiting for a trial period to end. The trial could never end, because nothing was collecting the observations. Rather than build the collector, we turned enforcement on, first on the test network and then in production, and added two more browser isolation headers alongside it. There is now also an automated check that fails the build if any of these headers is weakened or dropped, which is the part that actually keeps it true over time. We should be clear about one thing we chose not to tighten: the policy still allows images and video from any secure address, because that is what makes media in posts work at all. That breadth would matter only if hostile code were already running inside Mirage itself, and closing it would break inline media, so we left it and are telling you rather than pretending otherwise.

### Tested by breaking them on purpose, and honest about what is left

Every fix here ships with a test, and we checked those tests the only way that means anything: by putting each bug back and confirming the tests failed — forty-seven in total across the index, the site and the app, every one of them caught. The ones covering database behaviour run against a real database in a scratch area rather than merely inspecting the code, because a safeguard that is present but broken would satisfy the weaker check. For the app, that check now runs automatically on every change: it removes each of these fixes in turn and refuses to build unless a test notices, so none of them can be undone quietly later.

That process earned its keep on the standing fix above, which was wrong the first time. Every test for it passed, the repair ran, and the credit came back in full the next time the index restarted — an old startup routine, labelled as running once but in fact running every time, quietly restored the very rows the repair had just removed. Nothing in the tests covered what happens at startup, so nothing caught it; a whole-database consistency check did, during the release itself. Had it shipped as written, we would have told you this was fixed and it would have quietly stopped being true the first time a node was restarted. The duplicate routine is gone, and there is now a test that restarts the index for real and fails if the credit reappears.

Several known limitations stay on the record rather than being quietly dropped. Some profile information is still fetched from the chain while a block is being written, which we improved but did not eliminate, because removing it entirely would have meant duplicating logic that an earlier security fix deliberately consolidated in one place. When a node indexes old blocks, it still describes them using today's settings rather than the settings in force at the time, so replayed history can differ in detail from what was originally recorded. The admin statistics view can still forward one proof to several nodes in your own fleet, though it can no longer be tricked into forwarding it to a stranger's. There is still no ceiling on the network fee a node will pay to relay a message on your behalf, which we have looked at and decided to leave, because any cap low enough to be useful made the longest posts impossible to publish. And notifications are still sent to Apple and Google's delivery service without an access token, which we are flagging loudly at startup until one is issued — it means anyone who obtained a copy of the device list could send notifications to those devices. On the app side, the two known advisories in the routing library we use are left in place: neither can be reached the way Mirage is built, and the fix is a rewrite-scale upgrade, so we recorded them by name — a new advisory will still stop the build, only these two will not.

One deliberate exception on the chain side belongs on this list too. Sending tokens directly to one of the network's internal accounts, by typing its address as the destination, still succeeds and the tokens are unrecoverable. We looked at blocking it and decided not to: it takes a person going out of their way to type an address no interface offers, it harms nobody but themselves, and the one genuine concern it raised — that a balance sitting there could hide a shortfall elsewhere — is now answered directly, because every node continuously checks that it can cover every reserve it has promised.

Every node must be on this version. Unlike the last release, this one changes the rules of the chain itself: the checks a transaction must pass are different, a module has been removed, and a node still running the old build will not merely fall behind on the index — it will disagree about the chain and stop following it. It goes out as one coordinated upgrade of the whole fleet, and the upgrade was rehearsed end to end before release.
